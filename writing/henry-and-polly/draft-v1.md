# My great-great-great-great-grandfather's travels

Henry Halley was born in Fairfax County, Virginia, around 1786. His wife Polly — Mary "Polly" Patton, daughter of James Patton and Elizabeth Dinwiddie — was from Virginia too. They married there and at some point before 1807 moved west to Kentucky, settling in Clark County and Bourbon County in the Bluegrass region. Their first child was born in 1807. They stayed for twenty-six years.

---

Henry had fought in the War of 1812 — Private, 17th Regiment, Kentucky Militia. His service record was filed under "Holly Henry," with a cross-reference to "Halley Henry." The name was already ambiguous in official documents before he ever left the state.

By then he and Polly had seven children, all born in Kentucky, the oldest twenty-six and the youngest eight. In June of that year their daughter Elizabeth was still in Winchester, Clark County, with her husband and a new infant. That is the last confirmed Kentucky record. The next child born into the family appears in Macon County, Missouri, about a year later.

The route west ran along the Bee Trace — an ancient path through the high ground between Missouri's river drainages, used first by the Sac, Fox, and Iowa, then by the first wave of settlers from Kentucky and Virginia. The county they were heading for didn't exist yet. Macon County wasn't organized until January 1837.

The journey was about 190 years ago.

In Henry's wagon: Polly Ann, 17; Matthew, 15; John Patton, 13; George, 11; Dinwiddie, 8. Three old enough to remember the journey clearly. Two young enough that the wagon would be their first clear memory. Plus Henry and Polly, both in their late forties.

The oldest two children were already heads of their own households. James Patton, 26, had married Eleanor Fisher the year before; she was twenty and pregnant with their first child, who would be born in Missouri. Elizabeth, 21, traveled with her husband Frederick Rowland and their infant daughter Rebecca, born in Winchester that June. Three wagons, roughly thirteen or fourteen people, the Bee Trace, sometime around 1833 or 1834.

What they found: almost nothing. No county, no towns. Trails through high ground that the Osage had used before them. A community that would form slowly around a church that didn't exist yet — Friendship Baptist, where five of the seven children would eventually be buried.

Henry and Polly are not buried there. They are in a private plot in Macon County. Two graves. Nobody else.

John Patton Halley — the thirteen-year-old in the wagon — is buried at Mount Salem Cemetery in Excello, four miles from where they settled. So is his son John William, and his grandson William Edgar, and William Edgar's wife Mattie Stuck, who lived to ninety-three and could remember what the old people said. So are my father Raymond Allen Halley and my mother Frances Merle Davis Halley. Five generations in the same small cemetery, the oldest buried in 1881, the most recent in 2015. A hundred and thirty-four years.

Henry and Polly started it. They are the only two in their plot. The children spread out across four counties, and then the grandchildren, and then it settled.

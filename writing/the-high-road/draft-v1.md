# The High Road

*Draft v1, 3 Oct 2026. A scaffold for Alan to rewrite. Facts are from this session's research and Alan's own words; nothing added. Working title.*

**Dek:** How I went looking for the road Henry and Polly took to Missouri, and found out I'd been living on it.

---

I spent the winter of 1995 on my brother Dan's farm, just west of Highway 63, on the Randolph–Macon county line. A hundred acres. I walked the perimeter every day for six months. West of the farm the land dropped away. Nothing out there but deer and wild turkeys.

On Christmas Day my uncle Earl told me how the Halleys got to Macon County. The wagon broke down, and they stayed. He gave me directions: "Drive east from Excello a few miles and the Y's." The Y was Woodville. I drove there after he left.

I wrote that story down this year. Henry and Polly, three wagons, thirteen people, a broken axle. What I didn't have was the road.

## The confident answer

I asked an AI. It gave me a route with stations, like a train schedule. Land at Franklin on the Missouri River. North past Fayette to Huntsville, "the northernmost point of civilization." East to Moberly, onto the ridge. North past Jacksonville to College Mound and the Narrows, "a razor-thin ridge" where the wagons went single file. Then east to Cox.

It was the Bee Trace, it said. An ancient path. Used by the Sac, Fox and Iowa before the settlers.

I grew up in Huntsville. Part of it sounded right. Part of it didn't.

We lived a few miles west of Huntsville for a while, across the river. The road from town followed a ridge, then dropped into a valley that flooded. I crossed that river in a car more than once with water over the road. From our house the highway ran north to College Mound, high and dry. That's the road I knew. But Moberly to College Mound to Excello looked like a detour, and it crossed a river twice.

## Taking it apart

Two county histories mention the Bee Trace. The 1884 *History of Randolph and Macon Counties* gives it one paragraph: it entered Macon County "about the center of range 14" on the Grand Divide, and went up the ridge "through what was then called the Narrows, near the present site of Macon City." Waller's *History of Randolph County* (1920) quotes a settler named James Lamb, who arrived in 1837: "A road ran north and south through the township, called the 'Bee Trace,' so called from the fact that it was the route traveled by the old pioneers who hunted wild honey." Honey was worth twenty cents a gallon.

That's all. Nothing about Franklin. Nothing about Huntsville. Nothing about the Sac, Fox or Iowa. Nothing about where it started in the south.

The federal land survey put the center of range 14 on the crest of the ridge near Mount Salem. College Mound is in range 15, on a different ridge. The road I remembered was high and dry, all right. It just wasn't the Bee Trace.

Then the landing. Glasgow looks best on a modern map. It didn't exist until 1836, four years after Henry and Polly came. Boonville was on the south bank, the wrong side of the river. Old Franklin had flooded out by about 1828. Rocheport was on the north bank, had a ferry from 1819, and was platted in 1832, the year of the move.

Waller says emigrants could come "by boat to Hannibal or Glasgow and thence to their destination by land." Hannibal would mean coming from the east. Earl said they came up from the Missouri River.

I've driven from Rocheport to Macon many times, on four-lane highways. I wondered if that's why Rocheport looked right to me.

## The ridge

So I took the people out of it. USGS elevation data, about 3,100 points between the river and Excello. Find the highest continuous path from Rocheport, and from Franklin, without being told where the roads are.

From Rocheport it climbs straight north onto the divide in four miles and stays there. Past Moberly, past Jacksonville, through the Narrows, to Excello. Fifty-two miles. From Franklin it goes north past Fayette and east along another ridge to Moberly. Sixty-one miles, more climbing. Neither goes through Huntsville. Neither goes to College Mound.

Waller had already said it: Moberly is "the highest point between Hannibal and Brunswick." The railroad took that ridge in 1858. Highway 63 took it later. The four-lane wasn't why Rocheport looked right. The road and I had both followed the ridge.

I was born in Moberly. I grew up just off the ridge in Huntsville. The narrowest part of the divide, where the crest is under a mile wide, starts just north of Dan's farm. I walked that ridge every day for six months and didn't know it.

## The land

Henry got two federal land patents. April 1837, forty acres, with a land speculator named Joshua Gentry. August 1838, forty more, in his own name. Section 35, Township 56 North, Range 13 West.

The survey puts them about eight-tenths of a mile southeast of Woodville.

Henry and Polly were buried in the Halley Cemetery. I asked Nancy, who has photographed cemeteries all over Randolph and Macon counties. "The Halley Cemetery I'm afraid has been lost to time," she wrote. Much of the land has grown up into woods. Somebody on a four-wheeler came across some stones a long time ago. Nobody knows who. Cox, where the cemetery is usually said to be, was a post office from 1887 to 1907. It's gone too.

## Earl

Two county histories. A federal land survey. Three thousand elevation readings. A cemetery volunteer. An AI that was sure of itself.

Earl told me in one sentence.

"Drive east from Excello a few miles and the Y's."

France, 1982: Living There

Part 2 of 5.

France · 1982 · October 2026
Back to writing

I was given an English-French dictionary and a French document that described the project I was to work on. It was a finite element study of heat stress in nuclear power plant piping. I translated the document word by word. Took me three days.

Then I was taken downstairs to the computer room. A dark windowless room with 8 IBM 3270 terminals. Everything was in French! But I knew the screens in English and had no trouble learning to run the finite element codes. I learned something about finite element analysis and even wrote a small paper — in English. My internship was to last three months, but they asked me to stay another month, as I was actually productive (unlike other interns?).

I had a big office to myself on the second floor with a wall of glass looking onto wheat fields with some hills in the distance. I ate fabulous food in the cafeteria. There was a huge selection on the buffet tables (1,000 employees at Les Renardières with no restaurants nearby), and they would cook steak and fries if I liked. The lunch break was two hours. Instead of gossiping in French, I would go for a walk in the woods. I had asked why Les Renardières was located here. "Because it is on the Seine, and it is beautiful."

I needed to make a call to the US to get a computer tape with my programs.

"There's the phone," said my boss. "Just dial 7, then 1 for the US, and the number."

International phone calls were free at Les Renardières.

The Communist computer operator came to get my tape. He didn't speak English. He sneered at me. My boss explained his political conviction and that he despised Americans. He installed the tape in a few days, not the weeks that I feared.

They allowed me to run my nuclear codes on their IBM computers so I could continue to work on my thesis. They offered use of a Cray supercomputer, but I didn't know the Cray operating system, and I'd have to verify all my nuclear codes again on an unknown system, so I reluctantly declined the offer.

I could read a page of numbers on green bar paper as fast as I could flip the pages. Each run of Morse, the Monte Carlo shielding code I used for my thesis, generated one to three inches of paper. I had stacks and stacks of green bar in my big office at Les Renardières.

The French guys had big plotters. We didn't have any at UMC. I used them for the finite element analysis I did for them. But there was no plotting code for Morse then. I dreamed of drawing 3D shielding configurations on a computer and having the results plotted. In color.

After work, I would walk along the canal to Moret-sur-Loing and buy cheese and beer for a snack before dinner. I crossed the bridge over the river to the Moret portal, with two water mills and weeping willows and trout in the fast-flowing river below. Moret was a 12th-century walled town with a bakery, a creamery, a butcher, a horse meat shop, two fishing shops, and a gun shop. And one restaurant I could afford to eat in and several that were beyond my means. No tipping allowed at the cheap little restaurant in Moret. Not even the coins. I left the coins anyway.

From my third-floor bedroom I could watch the TGV cross high above the valley. And watch the boats go by in the canal below. The Peltier cooler came with my room.

Dinner was five courses, with red wine. The landlady discovered that I liked chocolate mousse, so she made it for me most days. And she made German desserts too. Some nights I had two desserts. And then we would retire to the parlor with the baby grand piano. One of the student tenants of the house could play anything he had ever heard. The landlady and I sipped cognac and listened to music. I quite liked living there. And I went into Paris most weekends.

I walked Paris many Saturdays. I learned the Metro so I didn't need the map. I went to Harry's Bar several times. I learned to like Pernod. French beer almost tasted good. I mostly just wandered the streets alone. Some British girls under the Arc de Triomphe wanted to marry me to get a US passport. I decided not to marry them. I had no money to eat in nice restaurants. I went to Versailles one day. I liked the gardens. I went to Fontainebleau several times. I liked the gardens and a crêperie there.

One Saturday in Paris I met an American couple in front of American Express. I had wine and cheese on the grass bank of the Seine with them. They were planning to get married. Her father bought them a summer in Europe to see if they could stand each other for three months. They had bought an old car in Luxembourg, driven down the east coast of the Adriatic to Greece and up through Italy to France, and were going home in a few days.

Together still?

"Oh, yes! Would you like to buy our car? We've driven it quite a ways, but there are rigid mechanical tests to renew a license in Europe, and the car still runs fine."

It was an old Nissan F-II, faded orange. $400. I went into American Express to get the $400, and drove my new purchase to Saint-Mammès.

I still took the Mercedes to work. And walked to Moret for whatever the landlady needed to prepare dinner. I drove the Nissan to Paris a few times. Not a problem. But I usually took the train.

The next Friday, after work, the landlady asked me if I would take her son to Bonn. His car had broken down, and he had planned to see his girlfriend this weekend. I told her that I had driven the car only from Paris and wasn't sure it would make it to Bonn. She insisted it would, so Jean-Pierre and I set off for Bonn.

We listened to the radio a while, then gradually discovered that he spoke fluent English. He wasn't sure.

We stopped at a town before the German border to spend the night in his grandfather's house. It was a row house, four floors high. There was an elevator in the house. It was a ten-foot-square floor that went up and down at the back of the house. To move his grandfather's handmade furniture. The house was full of huge, intricately carved furniture. We looked through some of the furniture but didn't find the gold coins that Jean-Pierre was sure were there.

We went down to the basement and found hundreds of bottles of wine covered with dust. And then we went down to a lower level. Then to a lower level.

"Places for people to hide during the wars," Jean-Pierre explained.

We went on to Bonn after a night at the grandfather's house. We stayed with the girlfriend's family. The girlfriend and two of her friends showed me and Jean-Pierre around Bonn. I had a real Budweiser at a bar. No problem with the Nissan.

The French border guards wanted to question me about the Luxembourg plates, my international driver's license and the French insurance card. Jean-Pierre intervened, and they left me for their next victim.

# France, 1982: Getting There

The France story began with the NE department secretary handing me some papers and telling me to "fill this out."

"What is it?" I asked.

"It's an application for a three-month internship in Germany, France or Japan."

"Why should I fill out the form?"

"Because you're the only one of the 27 students in the department who isn't married."

The NE department at Missouri was a graduate program, no undergraduates. So I filled out the form and forgot about it.

Two months later, the secretary told me that I had been accepted into the internship program and that I was going to Germany, France or Japan.

"Which one?"

"That hasn't been decided yet."

The destination was decided a few weeks later: I was going to France. When? July 1. Six weeks to prepare for France. Okay, where is France? I bought a Fodor's Europe, a paperback three inches thick. And I bought a book to study French that couldn't keep me awake.

Then the date was pushed back. My French boss decided to take his vacation in July instead of the usual August. I was staying at a friend's house that was empty for the summer, so I could stay there until August. And not learn any French.

I got my passport, bought two North Face bags, made arrangements for a place to stay in France, and flew to CDG. I rode an escalator in a glass tube and thought that I had arrived in the future. I wandered around lost for a bit, and someone took pity on the guy with two red bags looking around in a daze and asked if he could help me. I told him I was going south of Paris, to a town I couldn't pronounce, but I had the name printed on a paper note. I was directed to the train to Paris and told to take the Metro to Gare de Lyon.

Me and my two bags made it to Gare de Lyon, and I was lost again. Someone told me to take the elevator up to level zero. Sure enough, the elevator had a level zero, and I went up to a huge train station. I somehow bought a ticket to Moret-sur-Loing without being able to pronounce it.

A 45-minute train ride and I arrived in Moret. On a Sunday morning. The train station was deserted. No office open, no one standing anywhere. But there was a telephone that said Taxi, so I called and got a taxi to Saint-Mammès.

I walked through the unlocked iron gates and up some steps and knocked on the door. The woman who opened the door was surprised that the man there spoke no French. She hadn't spoken English in twenty years, but she had learned English in Germany. I told her my name, and yes, she was expecting me. She took me to my rented room on the third floor of her house.

The next morning was my first day of work at Les Renardières. A French student in another of the four third-floor rooms and I met the minibus to work. It was a Mercedes-Benz!

At work I had to sign some papers. Oops, I needed another paper. I needed to go to Paris to get it, now. I couldn't start work without insurance. So I was given a thick little book that contained a map of Paris. In the center was the Metro map. Looked complicated. I was given an address and shown how to look up the address in the book and just navigate the Metro to my destination. I returned to work a few hours later. The Paris map book worked quite well.

On my second day of work, I arrived at my new temporary home to find my landlady sitting on the front steps crying. Her dog had died. She said that she would never have another dog, as she or the dog could die and leave the other alone. She asked me to bury the dog in the backyard. "There's a shovel in the garage." I found the shovel and buried the dog at the far end of the sand bowling lane.

For burying the dog, I was invited to eat dinner with the landlady and her 18-year-old son. She had never allowed any of the interns who rented rooms in her house to eat with her. I would have to pay for my share of the food.

What did I like?

Filet mignon.

"Perfect, that's my son's favorite, too."

Filet mignon was the only French food I knew.
